# National Employment Authority: Interview Preparation and Job Seeker Services

## What a Job Interview Is

A job interview is a formal exchange between an employer and a candidate, used by the employer to assess whether the candidate can do the job, would fit the organization, and is the best match for the role — and used by the candidate to assess whether they'd want the job, can do it well, and whether it offers room to grow.

## Preparing for an Interview

Before your interview, make sure you genuinely know your own CV inside out, have researched the employer and the specific role, have confirmed the date, time, and location, and understand the interview format you're likely to face — since different formats call for different preparation.

## During the Interview: What to Do

Arrive on time or a little early. Dress appropriately for the industry you're interviewing in. Switch your phone off rather than to silent. Sit still, keep good posture, and avoid fidgeting. Speak clearly, and don't rush your answers — it's fine to take a moment to think before responding, and to ask for clarification if a question isn't clear. Keep your body language open and engaged. Stay positive when discussing your own experience. Prepare a few genuine questions to ask at the end. Frame your answers around what you can contribute to the employer, not what you hope to get from them. Afterward, thank the interviewer — in person, or with a brief follow-up email or note.

## During the Interview: What to Avoid

Don't arrive late. Don't swear or use slang. Don't come across as too relaxed or visibly uninterested. Don't lie about your experience. Don't chew gum, smoke beforehand, or show up smelling of alcohol. Don't act as though you've already got the job. Don't read directly from notes or your CV instead of speaking naturally. Don't speak negatively about former employers or colleagues. And don't argue with the interviewer, regardless of how strongly you disagree with something they've said.

## Questions Worth Asking the Interviewer

A few genuinely useful ones to have ready: whether the organization offers ongoing training or professional development, how they'd personally define success in this role, and when you can expect to hear back about next steps.

## Common Mistakes Candidates Make

The most frequent ones, according to NEA's guidance: arriving late, forgetting to switch off a phone, appearing disinterested, being overly self-promotional in a way that reads as arrogance rather than confidence, making jokes or swearing, not taking the opportunity to ask any questions, failing to clearly explain what you'd actually bring to the role, saying either too little or too much in your answers, dressing inappropriately for the setting, and poor eye contact throughout.

## Types of Interviews You Might Encounter

Phone interviews, one-on-one/face-to-face interviews, multi-stage interview processes, group interviews, panel interviews (multiple interviewers at once), video/teleconferencing interviews, informal meal-based interviews (breakfast, lunch, or dinner settings), and written interviews (assessments completed in writing rather than spoken).

## Registering as a Job Seeker with NEA

Beyond interview skills, the National Employment Authority also runs a formal job-seeker registration system through County Employment Bureaus located across Kenya. To register, you visit your local County Employment Bureau in person and bring your original academic and training certificates, identification documents, and any other relevant testimonials, along with copies for the office to retain. Once your documents are reviewed and verified, the County Employment Officer records your details in the official Jobseekers Register and issues you a Registration Card tied to your identification and an assigned Occupational Code. Importantly, this card needs to be renewed monthly for as long as you remain unemployed and actively seeking work — it isn't a one-time registration.

## What County Employment Bureaus and NEA Actually Offer

Through NEA and the County Employment Bureaus, Kenyans can access both local and foreign employment placement services — this includes being matched with prospective employers based on your registered qualifications, as well as support specifically for those seeking employment opportunities abroad. These services exist as a formal, government-run alternative to purely relying on private job boards or informal networks.
