# The Hidden Curriculum: Navigating Your First Job in Kenya

*A practical guide for first-generation professionals starting their first formal job in Kenya.*

## Understanding Probation in Practice

Probation is typically the first 3-6 months of your job, and its legal definition matters less day-to-day than how it actually feels. During this period, your manager is quietly forming an impression — not usually through formal check-ins, but through small signals: are you reliable, do you ask reasonable questions, do you follow through. Most Kenyan employers won't spell out exactly what "passing" probation looks like, so don't wait for a formal rubric. It's reasonable to ask your manager directly, around the halfway point, something like: "Is there anything you'd like to see more of from me before my probation review?" This isn't a sign of weakness — it shows initiative, and most managers respect being asked directly rather than guessing.

## Professional Communication Norms

Email tone in Kenyan offices tends to be warmer than Western corporate email, but still professional — a brief greeting ("Hi [Name]," or "Dear [Name],") is expected, not overly casual. In meetings, how directly you're expected to speak up varies by seniority of the room: in a small team meeting, contributing ideas is usually welcomed; in a large meeting with senior leadership present, it's often better to wait until asked or find a quieter moment afterward, unless you're specifically invited to speak. If you're waiting on a reply to an important email, it's acceptable to follow up after 2-3 working days — a short, polite nudge ("Just following up on this in case it got buried — happy to resend if useful") is normal, not pushy. If your workplace uses WhatsApp or Slack for internal communication, match the formality of how your team actually uses it — observe for the first week before assuming casual chat is fine.

## Addressing Colleagues and Seniors

Whether to use first names or titles depends heavily on the type of workplace. At more traditional employers — banks, government-adjacent organizations, older established companies — using "Mr./Mrs./Madam" plus surname, or a title like "Sir/Madam," is often expected until someone senior explicitly invites first-name use. At startups and many NGOs, first names are the norm from day one, even with the most senior person in the room. When in doubt in your first week, default to the more formal option and adjust once you see how others address that person.

## Dress Code and Professional Presentation

Formality varies significantly by employer type. Banks, insurance companies, and traditional corporates typically expect full formal wear — suits, closed shoes, minimal casual elements — especially in your first weeks. NGOs and development organizations are often smart-casual: neat, presentable, but without a strict suit requirement. Tech startups tend to be the most relaxed, sometimes genuinely casual day-to-day. If you don't know which category your new employer falls into, dress more formally than you think necessary on day one — it's far easier to relax your presentation once you've seen the actual office norm than to walk back an overly casual first impression. By the end of week one, you should have a clear read on what's expected; adjust from there.

## Salary, Pay, and Money Conversations

Salary is commonly paid via bank transfer, though M-Pesa is sometimes used, particularly at smaller employers — confirm which applies to you during onboarding, and get it in writing if it isn't already in your offer letter. Your payslip will show statutory deductions: NSSF (National Social Security Fund, a retirement contribution), NHIF/SHA (health insurance contribution), and PAYE (Pay As You Earn, income tax) — these are standard and not something to question, but you're entitled to understand what each deduction is for, and asking HR to walk you through your first payslip is completely normal. Negotiating salary as a first-time entry-level hire is limited but not unheard of — if you do it, focus on demonstrated skills or competing offers rather than personal financial need, and keep the conversation brief and professional rather than treating it as a debate.

## Time and Punctuality Expectations

There's a real difference between social "African time" norms and workplace punctuality expectations, and conflating the two is a common early mistake. For scheduled meetings, interviews, and your actual start time, arrive on time or a few minutes early — this is taken seriously at almost every formal employer, regardless of how relaxed the general office culture feels. Social events organized by colleagues (after-work gatherings, informal lunches) tend to run on a looser, more flexible timeline, and showing up exactly on the dot isn't expected the same way. When unsure which category something falls into, treat it as formal until you've seen otherwise.

## Asking for Help Without Seeming Incompetent

Many first-generation professionals hold back questions out of fear it signals they don't belong — this is one of the most common and most damaging instincts to unlearn early. In reality, asking a clear, specific question after having genuinely tried to figure something out first is read as competence, not weakness. A useful pattern: "I looked into this and tried X, but I'm not fully sure about Y — could you point me in the right direction?" This shows effort while still getting you the help you need. Struggling silently for days out of pride costs you far more credibility than a well-framed question ever will.

## Networking and Mentorship

Without a family member who's navigated a corporate career, you likely don't have a built-in professional network the way some colleagues do — this is a real structural gap, not a personal failing, and it's worth actively addressing rather than waiting for it to happen naturally. Start small: identify one or two colleagues whose work you respect, and build the relationship through genuine work interactions first — asking their advice on a task, thanking them for useful feedback — rather than an artificial "can I pick your brain" request out of nowhere. A mentor relationship usually grows out of repeated small interactions, not a single formal ask.

## Visibility, LinkedIn, and Upskilling

Doing good work quietly is not, on its own, enough — the people who make decisions about your growth need to actually know what you've contributed, which means learning to communicate your work without it feeling like bragging. A simple habit: at the end of a project, send a short update to your manager summarizing what you did and its outcome, framed as information-sharing, not self-promotion. On LinkedIn, Kenyan recruiters and employers do genuinely use the platform — keep your profile current, connect with colleagues and people in your field, and share occasional posts about work you're proud of or things you're learning; you don't need to post constantly, but an empty or outdated profile is a missed opportunity. For upskilling, look for low-cost or free options first — Ajira Digital offers training relevant to digital and remote work skills, and many employers have some budget for relevant courses if you ask directly rather than assuming there's none available.

## Red Flags vs. Normal Discomfort

Most of what feels unfamiliar in your first months — not knowing the unwritten rules, feeling unsure how to phrase something, wondering if you're doing enough — is simply normal adjustment, not a sign something is wrong. It's worth distinguishing that from genuine red flags: being asked to work consistently without pay during a "trial period," an employer who is vague or evasive about your actual contract terms, or being treated in a way that violates basic workplace rights. Kenya's Employment Act provides formal legal protections around contracts, working hours, and termination — if something feels like it may cross from "unfamiliar" into "actually not right," it's worth checking your rights directly rather than assuming discomfort alone means something is wrong.

## Glossary

**Probation** — an initial trial period at the start of employment, typically 3-6 months, during which either party can usually end the employment more easily than after it's completed.

**NSSF** — National Social Security Fund; a mandatory retirement savings contribution deducted from your salary.

**NHIF / SHA** — National Hospital Insurance Fund / Social Health Authority; mandatory health insurance contributions.

**PAYE** — Pay As You Earn; income tax deducted directly from your salary each pay period.

**Appraisal** — a formal review of your performance, usually conducted periodically (e.g., annually or after probation ends).

**KPI** — Key Performance Indicator; a specific, measurable target used to assess how well you're doing in a role.

**Notice period** — the amount of time, specified in your contract, that you or your employer must give before ending your employment.
