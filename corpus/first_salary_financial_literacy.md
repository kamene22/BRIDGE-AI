# First Salary and Financial Literacy Guide for Young Kenyans

*A practical financial guide for young professionals navigating their first payslip, understanding statutory deductions, and managing entry-level income in Kenya.*

---

## Deconstructing Your First Payslip

When you receive your first formal salary, the amount deposited in your bank or M-Pesa account (your **Net Pay**) will be lower than the salary stated in your offer letter (your **Gross Pay**). The difference goes to mandatory government taxes and contributions, known as **statutory deductions**.

### 1. PAYE (Pay As You Earn)
PAYE is the direct income tax levied by the Kenya Revenue Authority (KRA) on your earnings.
- **How it works:** It is calculated on a progressive scale. Higher portions of your income are taxed at higher rates (starting at 10% and scaling up to 30% or more for high income brackets).
- **Personal Relief:** All resident individuals are entitled to a monthly personal tax relief (currently KES 2,400), which is subtracted from your calculated PAYE to reduce your tax burden.

### 2. NSSF (National Social Security Fund)
NSSF is a mandatory government retirement savings scheme.
- **How it works:** Contributions are divided into Tier I and Tier II rates based on your pensionable earnings. The maximum standard deduction from your salary is KES 1,080 per month, with your employer matching the same amount.

### 3. NHIF / SHA (Social Health Authority)
This is the mandatory national health insurance contribution.
- **How it works:** Formerly tiered under NHIF, contributions under the Social Health Authority (SHA) are set at a standard rate of **2.75% of your gross monthly salary**. This provides medical coverage at registered public and private healthcare facilities.

### 4. Affordable Housing Levy (AHL)
A statutory deduction introduced to fund national housing projects.
- **How it works:** Set at **1.5% of your gross monthly salary**, which is matched by your employer (an additional 1.5%).

## Basic Budgeting: The 50/30/20 Rule (Kenya Context)

A standard starting framework for managing your net salary is the **50/30/20 Rule**:

1. **50% for Needs (Essential Living Expenses):**
   - Rent and service charges.
   - Commute expenses (Matatu fares, fuel, or ride-hailing).
   - Basic groceries and utilities (electricity/token, water, gas, home internet).
   - Basic SHA/medical cover.
2. **30% for Wants (Personal Spending & Lifestyle):**
   - Dining out, entertainment, and hobbies.
   - Clothes and non-essential shopping.
   - Subscriptions (Spotify, Netflix).
3. **20% for Savings and Debt Repayment:**
   - Building an emergency fund.
   - Sacco savings (Highly recommended in Kenya for dividends and low-interest loans).
   - Paying off student loans (e.g., HELB) or personal debts.

## Saving and Investing Options for Young Professionals

### 1. Saccos (Savings and Credit Co-operatives)
Saccos are one of the most effective saving and borrowing vehicles in Kenya.
- **Benefits:** They offer high interest rates on deposits (dividends) and allow you to borrow up to 3 or 4 times your savings at stable, lower-interest rates (usually 1% per month on a reducing balance). Look for registered, stable Saccos (e.g., Stima Sacco, Harambee Sacco, Safaricom Sacco).

### 2. Money Market Funds (MMFs)
An MMF is a low-risk investment fund that pools money from investors to purchase short-term securities.
- **Benefits:** They pay daily interest (compounded monthly, averaging 9% to 15% annually depending on market conditions). They are highly liquid, meaning you can withdraw your money within 2 to 3 days, making them perfect for your Emergency Fund. Popular MMFs in Kenya include CIC, Sanlam, Britam, and Co-op Trust.

### 3. Government Treasury Bills (T-Bills) and Bonds
For long-term, secure investing, you can buy government debt. T-bills run for short terms (91, 182, or 364 days), while Treasury Bonds run for several years. These are managed via the Central Bank of Kenya (CBK) DhowCSD platform.

## Common Financial Traps to Avoid

### 1. Lifestyle Inflation
When your income increases, it is tempting to immediately upgrade your lifestyle (moving to a more expensive apartment, buying costlier clothes, or eating out at expensive restaurants). This trap keeps you living paycheck to paycheck regardless of how much you earn. Treat upgrades as planned rewards, not automatic reactions to a raise.

### 2. Mobile Loan App Traps
Mobile app loans (e.g., Tala, Branch, Zenka) are incredibly easy to access but carry high interest rates (often 10% to 20% interest for a 30-day loan, which scales to over 120% APR). Relying on these apps for lifestyle spending leads to a cycle of debt. Avoid them unless dealing with a critical life-or-death emergency.

### 3. Unregulated Investment Schemes (Chamas / Pyramid Schemes)
If an investment scheme promises high, guaranteed monthly returns (e.g., "earn 30% returns per month on your money") or requires you to recruit new members to earn commissions, it is a scam. Stick to regulated products licensed by the Capital Markets Authority (CMA) or the Sacco Societies Regulatory Authority (SASRA).
